> **Key takeaways**
> - There is no right number. A good week still has room for homework, meals, sleep and free play.
> - Watch for signs of a week that is too full, and of one that is too empty.
> - One example pattern: one active afternoon, one creative, one free.
> - Count the drive at pickup time, and plan siblings' weeks together.
> - Let your child help choose, and try one session before a whole term.

Some weeks look great on the calendar and feel awful in the car. Others are so open that by Wednesday everyone is bored. This guide is for Southern California parents of kids aged 4 to 14 who want the middle: enough going on, and enough room left over.

## There is no magic number

Parents often hope for a single number. There isn't one. A 5-year-old worn out after kindergarten may be happiest with one short class a week. A 12-year-old with a sport and an instrument may enjoy more. Two siblings can need very different weeks.

A better question: does the week still have room? Room for homework, dinner together, a steady bedtime, and one afternoon with nothing planned. If yes, and your child still looks forward to their activities, you are probably close.

## Too full or too empty? Signs to watch

| The week may be too full when... | The week may be too empty when... |
|---|---|
| Your child dreads activities they used to like | Most afternoons feel restless and bored |
| Homework gets pushed late or done in the car | Screens fill the time because nothing else is on |
| Evenings are tired and short-tempered | Your child keeps asking to do more |
| There is no time left for friends or free play | There is energy to burn and nowhere to put it |

These are everyday signs, not a medical checklist. Every child has off weeks, so look for a pattern. If you are worried about your child's mood, sleep or health, talk to your child's doctor.

## Sketch the week before you fill it

Before you look at a single class, draw the week as it already is: five columns, Monday to Friday. Block in what doesn't change: pickup times for each child, homework, dinner, bedtime, work hours and who can drive which day. What's left is the real space you have. It is often smaller than it looks.

## One way to shape it: active, creative, free

Here is one example pattern some families like. It is a starting point, not a rule.

- **One active afternoon:** a team sport, dance, martial arts, swimming.
- **One creative afternoon:** art, music, cooking, building, coding.
- **One free afternoon:** nothing booked. Park, backyard, a friend over.

The other two weekdays stay light. A 5-year-old might do only one of the booked afternoons. A 13-year-old might add a second active day in their sport's season. Some families put the creative slot on a weekend morning. Swap the pieces until the week fits.

## Drive time and siblings

In Southern California, distance is measured in minutes, not miles, and after school is when the roads are slowest. A short hop at noon can be a long crawl at pickup time, so try the drive once at the real hour before you commit.

- Look close to school or home first. A good class nearby often beats a great one across town.
- Put siblings' activities on the same afternoon, ideally at the same place.
- Choose one class siblings can share, if the age range fits both.
- Share the drive with another family from the class.
- Try an online class for one slot. No drive at all.

With more than one child, plan the whole family's week, not each child's alone. Three kids with two activities each is six pickups, and someone is always waiting in the car.

## Protect downtime and sleep

Free time is not wasted time. Unplanned play is where kids make up games, get bored and work out what to do about it. An afternoon with nothing booked is part of the plan, not a gap in it.

A busy week also eats into sleep. Watch for late classes that push dinner and bedtime back. If bedtime keeps slipping on activity nights, rethink the timing rather than rushing faster. Your child's doctor can tell you how much sleep suits their age.

## Let your child help choose

Kids often stick with things longer when they had a say.

- **Ages 4 to 6:** offer two options and let them pick.
- **Ages 7 to 10:** ask what they want to try, and talk through which day and how long.
- **Ages 11 to 14:** let them make a real case, including how it fits around homework.

Agree at the start how long they will give something new. A few weeks is fair. One hard day is not enough to judge.

## When to drop or add something

Look at the whole week again every few months. It may be time to **drop an activity** if the dread lasts for weeks, if it keeps crowding out sleep or homework, or if your child has lost interest. Dropping something is making room, not giving up.

It may be time to **add one** if your child keeps asking or the free afternoons have turned long and bored. Add one thing at a time and let the week settle. A swap often beats a straight add: one in, one out.

## Try one session before a whole term

Before signing up for a full term, try a single session if you can. One visit tells you whether your child came out talking about it, whether the drive worked at that hour, and whether it left them buzzing or wiped out.

## FAQ

### How many activities should my child do?

There is no single right number. Start with one or two, keep at least one afternoon free, and watch how evenings and moods feel over a few weeks.

### What are signs my child is overscheduled?

Dread before activities they used to enjoy, homework pushed late, worn-out evenings and no time for friends or free play. Look for a pattern, not one bad day.

### Should siblings do the same activity?

It saves driving if the age range fits and both want it. Each child usually wants something of their own too, so a mix often works.
