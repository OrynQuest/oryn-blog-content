Some kids dance in the kitchen while you cook. Others only move when nobody is watching. Either way, a good dance class can give a child rhythm, confidence and a group to belong to. In Glendale, where many cultural communities keep folk dance part of family life, there is a lot to choose from.

## Start with how your child likes to move

Style names blur together on a schedule. Think instead about what each one feels like.

| Style | What it feels like | Often suits kids who |
|---|---|---|
| Ballet | Calm, structured and precise, with barre work first | Like routines and getting things just right |
| Tap | Loud and playful: the shoes are the instrument | Drum on every table |
| Jazz | Bright and showy, with kicks, turns and leaps | Have big energy and enjoy performing |
| Hip hop | Groovy and social, with room for personal style | Copy dances from videos |
| Contemporary | Expressive and flowing, often barefoot | Like telling a story with movement |
| Cultural and folk dance | Group dances passed down through communities, often in lines or circles | Love music from home and dancing with others |

Many studios also offer combination classes for young children, such as ballet and tap together, a gentle way to try two styles.

## How old is old enough?

There is no single right age. Many studios offer creative movement for preschoolers, built around music and games. Structured ballet, tap and jazz often begin in the early elementary years, and hip hop and contemporary tend to start a little older. Folk groups often mix a wide range of ages.

Readiness matters more than age. Can your child follow a short instruction and stay with a group for most of a class? Then they are probably ready to try. For questions about health or development, talk with your pediatrician.

## Recreational or competitive: two different commitments

Most children start in recreational classes. These usually meet once a week, focus on learning and fun, and end the season with a recital.

Competitive teams are a different path. Dancers often join by audition and train several times a week, with weekend competitions, sometimes out of town. Costumes, entry fees and travel add up.

Neither path is better. If your child asks about a team, talk with the teacher about what it involves first.

## Inside the studio: four things to notice

**Class size.** Smaller groups mean more chances for the teacher to correct a foot or a posture. Ask whether there is an assistant for the youngest ages.

**The floor.** Dancers jump, land and slide. A floor built for dance usually has some give, often with a smooth vinyl surface on top. Ask what is underneath.

**Observation policy.** Some studios have a viewing window. Others keep doors closed so kids can focus, then hold watch weeks a few times a year. Both can work; just know which before the first class.

**Recital expectations.** Ask when the recital is, how many rehearsals come before it, and what families pay for. Costumes, tickets and photos are common extra costs, so ask for the full list early.

## What happens at a first class

Most classes follow a similar rhythm: a warm-up, steps practiced in place (at the barre for ballet), travelling across the floor in small groups, then a short combination set to music. Many end with a cool-down or, in ballet, a bow to the teacher.

Young children may need a few weeks to settle in, and tears at the door are common. If your child holds back, ask whether they can watch for a few minutes first. ## What to wear, and what to wait on buying

Dress codes vary by style and studio, so ask before you shop.

- **Ballet:** often a leotard, tights and soft ballet slippers, with hair pulled back off the neck.
- **Tap:** tap shoes and comfortable, fitted clothes.
- **Jazz:** fitted clothes and soft jazz shoes.
- **Hip hop:** loose clothes and clean sneakers kept for indoors.
- **Contemporary:** fitted clothes and bare feet or half-sole shoes.
- **Cultural and folk dance:** comfortable clothes; costumes come later, for performances.

For a first class, comfortable clothes and the right shoes are usually enough.

## Questions to ask before you enroll

1. Which class would you place my child in, and why?
2. How many dancers are in the class, and how many adults?
3. Can parents watch, and when?
4. Is there a recital, and what does it involve in time and extra costs?
5. What should my child wear and bring to the first class?

## FAQ

### What is the right age to start dance classes?

Many studios offer creative movement for preschoolers and structured classes from the early elementary years. Readiness matters more than an exact age.

### Which dance style is best for a beginner?

Any style can work. Ballet builds structure, tap builds rhythm, and hip hop feels relaxed and social. A combination class lets young children try two.

### Do kids have to perform in a recital?

It depends on the studio. Many recreational programs end with a recital. Ask early about rehearsals, costumes and tickets.

### What is the difference between recreational and competitive dance?

Recreational classes usually meet once a week and focus on fun. Competitive teams train several times a week and travel to weekend competitions, which takes much more time and money.
