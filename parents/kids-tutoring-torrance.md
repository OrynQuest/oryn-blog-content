By late fall, most Torrance families have a feel for how the school year is going. Maybe a conference raised a question, or homework has turned into a nightly standoff. Or maybe your child is breezing through and wants more. This guide is for South Bay parents of kids aged about 5 to 14 who are wondering whether a tutor would help, and how to pick one.

## Is it time for extra help?

Every child has off weeks. One low grade is not a reason to panic. What matters is a pattern, such as:

- **Homework that used to be quick now takes the whole evening**, often with tears.
- **Your child talks down about a subject**: "I'm just bad at math."
- **Avoiding the work**, like leaving the book at school or rushing to be done.
- **The teacher mentions it**, or a skill on the progress report isn't moving.
- **A gap from last year** that new work builds on, like shaky times tables under fractions.
- **The other direction**: a child who finishes fast, seems bored and asks for harder problems.

Tutoring is about schoolwork and confidence. If your worries go beyond school, such as attention, mood or sleep, talk with the school and your child's doctor.

## Talk to the teacher before you search

The teacher sees your child work every day, next to classmates of the same age. A short chat or email can save weeks of guessing. Ask:

- Which specific skills need work? "Word problems" is something a tutor can target; "math" is not.
- What does my child do well? A good tutor builds from strengths.
- What is the class working on for the rest of the term?
- How can I help at home without doing the work for them?

Write down two or three clear goals from that talk. They are your yardstick later, and the first thing to share with a tutor.

## Kinds of tutoring, matched to what you see

There is no single right kind of tutoring. Use this as a rough guide.

| What you notice | Help that often fits |
|---|---|
| Missed assignments, homework fights | Homework help or study skills |
| Stuck in reading or math | One-on-one work on that subject |
| Shy or distracted in groups | One-on-one, often in person |
| Likes friends, needs a push | A small group |
| Older child facing a big test | Test prep |
| Ahead and bored | Enrichment |

**Homework help** keeps the week under control. A **reading or math focus** goes back to the skills under the homework, which feels slower but often lasts longer. **Test prep** suits older kids with a specific test ahead; younger children usually get more from steady skill building.

Also decide between **catch-up and enrichment**. Catch-up fills gaps so your child can keep pace in class. Enrichment stretches a child who is ready for more, with things like math puzzles or creative writing. Saying which one you want makes the search easier.

## In person or online?

**In person** often suits younger kids, who tend to focus better with someone beside them, and kids who learn with their hands. The trade-off is the drive: a short hop across Torrance can take much longer after school.

**Online** saves that drive. It works best with a quiet spot, a working camera and a child who can sit with a screen. For younger kids, keep an adult within earshot at first. Not sure? Try one of each and notice which session your child talks about afterward.

## Three questions to ask any tutor

1. **How do you find where my child is starting?** Listen for a plan: a short skills check, a look at recent schoolwork or a chat with your child.
2. **How will you share progress, and how often?** A short note after each session, or an update every few weeks, shows whether things are moving toward your goals.
3. **What does a typical session look like?** A clear shape, such as a warm-up, focused practice and a quick wrap-up, helps kids settle in.

## Keep it positive at home

- **Call it a coach, not a punishment.** Athletes and musicians work one-on-one too.
- **Don't drop the activity they love** to make room. It is often what keeps them going.
- **Praise effort and strategies**, like "you tried two ways to solve that", more than grades.
- **Plan for energy.** A snack and a short break after school help a lot.

## Try one session, then give it a few weeks

Start with a single session. Afterward, ask your child how it felt, and ask the tutor what they noticed and plan next. A first meeting tells you about fit, not results: did your child come out calm, and did the tutor explain a plan you understood? Our guide to [what a great first class looks like](/blog/what-a-great-first-class-actually-looks-like-and-what-to-ask) has more signs to watch.

If it feels right, give it a few weeks. Early signs are small: less resistance at homework time, a child who explains a problem out loud. Then check in with the teacher against your goals. If it still isn't working after a fair try, it's fine to change tutors or the kind of help.

## FAQ

### What age can kids start tutoring?

There's no set age. Younger children often do well with short, playful sessions on reading or number skills. Older kids can handle longer sessions and test prep.

### Is one-on-one tutoring better than a small group?

Neither is better for every child. One-on-one suits kids who need their own pace. Small groups suit social kids who work harder alongside others.

### How long before tutoring makes a difference?

Give it a few weeks. Look for small signs first, like calmer homework time, then check in with the teacher against your goals.
