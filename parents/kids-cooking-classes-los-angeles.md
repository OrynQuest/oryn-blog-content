> **Key takeaways**
> - Cooking classes teach measuring, following steps in order, safe knife skills at the right age, and a willingness to try new foods.
> - A safe class starts with handwashing, keeps groups small, and has an adult close by for anything sharp or hot.
> - Raise food allergies and dietary needs with the teacher before you book, not at the door.
> - The real test is at home: a child who wants to make the recipe again had a good class.

A kids' cooking class looks simple: aprons, flour, something in the oven. Inside, your child is reading a recipe, counting, sharing a counter and tasting something new. This guide follows a class in three stages: before you book, inside the kitchen, and after your child comes home.

## Before you book

### Match the tasks to your child's age

Every teacher runs the kitchen differently, but most give children jobs that fit their hands and attention span. In broad terms:

**Preschoolers (about 3 to 5)** wash fruit and vegetables, stir, pour ingredients that were measured ahead, tear herbs, spread soft toppings and decorate. The joy is in touching and mixing.

**Early grade schoolers (about 6 to 8)** start measuring with cups and spoons, cracking eggs, kneading dough and cutting soft foods like bananas or cooked potatoes with a kid-safe knife. They can follow a short recipe with a grown-up reading along.

**Older kids (about 9 to 12)** can follow a full recipe and, in many classes, use a real knife and the stove with an adult close by.

Ask what your child will do with their own hands. If kids mostly watch the teacher cook, it is a demonstration, not a cooking class.

### Allergies and dietary needs come first

If your child has a food allergy, an intolerance or a dietary need (vegetarian, halal, kosher, gluten-free), sort it out before you book. Ask:

- What ingredients are in the recipes for the session you want?
- Can a recipe be adapted for my child, and who prepares that version?
- How are shared counters, bowls and tools cleaned between uses?
- Do children share or swap food during class?
- If my child has an emergency plan from their doctor, who in the room will know it?

A good teacher answers these plainly. If you are left unsure, keep looking. This guide is general information, not medical advice; follow your child's doctor on anything to do with allergies.

### A short checklist for the teacher

- [ ] What age range is the class for, and how are tasks split by age?
- [ ] How many children are in a group, and how many adults?
- [ ] When do kids use knives, and what kind?
- [ ] Is there a stove or oven, and who is next to it?
- [ ] Do parents stay, wait nearby, or drop off?

## Inside the kitchen

### What kids actually learn

**Measuring.** Cups, spoons, halves and quarters stop being abstract when a cookie depends on them. Kids learn why a level spoon matters.

**Following steps in order.** A recipe is a set of instructions where the order counts. Reading ahead and gathering ingredients first are habits that carry beyond the kitchen.

**Knife safety at the right age.** Younger kids usually start with butter knives or kid-safe knives on soft foods. Older kids learn grips like the "claw", where fingertips tuck back, and the "bridge", where the hand arches over the food. A good teacher shows each grip slowly, then watches each child try it.

**Trying new foods.** Kids who chop and stir an ingredient are often keener to taste it. A good class invites a taste and never forces one.

### What a safe class looks like

When you visit or drop off, notice:

1. **Handwashing at the start**, and again after handling raw eggs or meat.
2. **Tools sized for kids**: smaller knives, step stools at the right height, mixing bowls a child can hold.
3. **Small groups**, so an adult can see every pair of hands.
4. **An adult next to anything hot or sharp**, every time, not across the room.
5. **Allergy questions asked up front**, in writing or in conversation, before children start cooking.

## After class

### What comes home

Most kids' cooking classes send children home with something: a container of what they made, a recipe card, or both. Ask about the class in the car. What they remember tells you what they learned.

### Cooking together at home

The class plants the idea; the home kitchen is where it grows:

- **Make the class recipe again** within a week, and let your child lead.
- **Give one job, start to finish.** Washing the salad, done fully, builds more confidence than a dozen half-tasks.
- **Shop together.** Many Los Angeles neighborhoods have a weekly farmers' market; let your child pick one new vegetable.
- **Cook your family's own dishes.** Teaching your child a recipe from home pairs well with whatever they learn in class.
- **Keep the class's safety rules at home** so the habits stick.

## FAQ

### What age can kids start cooking classes?

Many kids' cooking classes start around age 3 to 5 with simple jobs like washing, stirring and decorating. Measuring and kid-safe knife work usually come from about age 6, and full recipes later.

### Are kids' cooking classes safe for children with food allergies?

It depends on the class and the allergy. Message the teacher before you book to ask about ingredients, adapted recipes and how tools are cleaned, and follow your child's doctor's advice. This is general information, not medical advice.

### Do kids use real knives in cooking classes?

Younger kids usually use butter knives or kid-safe knives on soft foods. Older kids may use real knives with an adult close by. Ask each class how knife skills are taught.

### What should my child bring to a cooking class?

Ask the teacher. Many classes provide aprons and ingredients. Closed-toe shoes and long hair tied back are common requests.
