> **Key takeaways**
> - For ages 2 to 5, the routine of a class matters more than the subject.
> - Decide early: a class where you stay, or one where you say goodbye at the door.
> - Shorter is better at this age. Plan the class around the nap, never through it.
> - A good early-years teacher repeats, sings, gets down low and lets shy children watch.
> - Judge a class after a few visits. The first one is often just looking around.

Many Burbank families start looking for a first class somewhere between two and five. The choice is less about the perfect subject and more about the right shape of class for a very small person.

## First question: what will your child actually do?

Most early classes fall into a few families. Names vary, so read the description, not just the title.

**Music and movement.** Singing, shakers and drums, clapping games, moving to music. Easy for younger toddlers, because nobody expects them to sit still.

**Tumbling and movement play.** Rolling, crawling through tunnels, balancing on low beams, jumping onto soft mats. A good fit for climbers and runners.

**Art and messy play.** Paint, dough, glue and stickers. At this age the point is the squishing and mixing, not the finished picture.

If your child already has a favorite way to play, start there. A toddler who drums on every pot will probably enjoy music more than a painting table.

## Second question: will you stay or go?

This is the biggest practical choice.

In a **parent-and-child class**, you are in the room and part of the activity. You help with the song, catch your child at the bottom of the slide and step out with them if it is all too much. These suit younger toddlers and children new to being apart from you.

In a **drop-off class**, you say goodbye and come back later. These usually suit older preschoolers who already spend time at preschool, daycare or with a sitter. Some drop-off classes ask that children are toilet-trained, so ask before you book.

Neither is the more grown-up choice. Go with the child you have.

## Third question: how long, and when?

Short attention spans are normal at this age. Early classes are usually short, and a good one changes activity every few minutes. A class with long stretches of sitting and listening is built for older kids.

Timing matters just as much. Many two- and three-year-olds still nap in the middle of the day, and many four- and five-year-olds are dropping that nap. A class that cuts into nap time often goes badly, however good the teacher is. Morning classes suit a lot of toddlers, and in Burbank they get you home before the valley afternoon warms up. For a child who naps after lunch, a late-afternoon slot can work once they are rested and fed.

Bring a snack and water, arrive a few minutes early so your child is not rushed into a new room, and keep the rest of the day light after a first class.

## What a good class looks like, start to finish

Watch one session from the side if you can. Here is the shape to look for.

1. **Arrival.** The teacher greets each child by name at their eye level. Late arrivals are welcomed in, not scolded.
2. **Hello song or circle.** The same opening every week. Repetition is what makes a room feel safe to a small child.
3. **The main activity.** Short bursts with simple directions. Children who wander are invited back, not chased. A child who only watches is allowed to watch.
4. **A calm-down.** Something slower before the end: a quiet song, a stretch, a story.
5. **Goodbye.** A clear ending ritual, then a quick word with parents.

Notice the small moves too: offering two choices instead of orders, and staying calm through a meltdown.

For a wider look at the first visit, see [What a great first class actually looks like](/blog/what-a-great-first-class-actually-looks-like-and-what-to-ask).

## The goodbye at the door

Expect some tears at first. Being upset when a parent leaves is normal, and it usually eases as the class becomes familiar.

- **Keep goodbyes short and the same each time.** A hug, a set phrase, then go.
- **Don't sneak out.** It can make the next drop-off harder.
- **Say when you will be back in your child's terms.** "After the goodbye song" means more than a time on the clock.
- **Let the teacher take over.** A good one already has a plan for this.

If your child is still very upset after several weeks, talk with the teacher. For wider worries about how your child is doing, ask your child's doctor.

## Questions to ask before you book

| Ask | A good answer sounds like |
|---|---|
| What happens in a typical session? | A clear routine, not just a list of activities |
| Is this parent-and-child or drop-off? | A plain answer, plus what happens if your child needs you |
| What if my child just watches? | That's fine, and common at the start |
| How many children are in a group, and how many adults? | A number they can tell you |
| Do children need to be toilet-trained? | A clear yes or no |

## Try one session first

Before you commit to a full term anywhere, try a single session, then give it time. A first visit spent holding a parent's leg tells you little about the third. Watch for small signs at home: humming the hello song, asking about "the drum class". If it still isn't working after a few visits, a different kind of class, a different time of day or waiting a few months is a fine answer.

## FAQ

### What age can toddlers start classes?

Many parent-and-child classes welcome children from around two. Drop-off classes usually start a little later. Check the age range a class gives and ask the teacher if you are unsure.

### Should I stay with my child or drop them off?

Stay if your child is young or new to being apart from you. Drop-off tends to suit older preschoolers used to other adults.

### What if my child just watches the whole class?

That's normal at first. Watching is how many young children join in.
