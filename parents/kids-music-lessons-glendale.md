> **Key takeaways**
> - Start with your child, not the instrument. Temperament matters as much as age.
> - Private lessons go at your child's pace; group classes add friends and energy. Neither is the right answer for every child.
> - Judge the teacher in the first lesson: your child should play something real before it ends.
> - Short, regular practice beats long weekend sessions, and you are the audience, not the referee.
> - Rent or borrow while your child is still growing, and ask the teacher before you buy anything.

A child who taps on every table or sings in the car is telling you something. For Glendale parents, the hard part is not deciding to start music lessons. It is choosing the instrument, the kind of lesson and the teacher, then keeping it going once the new wears off. Here are those choices in the order you will face them.

## Step 1: Start with your child, not the instrument

Age matters, but temperament often matters more. Think about how your child already plays.

**The careful builder.** Likes puzzles, building blocks and getting things right. **Piano** is a natural fit. Every note is laid out in front of them, and the note reading carries over to other instruments. Many children start in the early elementary years.

**The patient perfectionist.** Will repeat a cartwheel until it is perfect. **Violin** comes in small sizes, so young children can begin, but the first months sound scratchy. It rewards kids who enjoy slow progress.

**The strummer and singer-along.** Wants to play songs they already know. **Ukulele** gives quick wins: small, four soft nylon strings, a happy sound early. **Guitar** often follows once hands are bigger. Expect sore fingertips at first.

**The drummer on every surface.** Has energy to spare and a strong beat. **Drums** channel that and teach counting. If you share walls with neighbors, a practice pad or an electronic kit with headphones helps.

**The performer.** Sings constantly and loves an audience. **Voice** needs no gear. Many teachers start younger children with group singing and save formal voice training for later, so ask what fits your child's age.

If two instruments both fit, let your child choose. A child who picked the instrument is more likely to stick with it.

## Step 2: Private lessons or a group class?

**Private lessons** suit children who are focused, a little shy, or already ahead. The teacher can fix a wrist the moment it slips, and your child sets the pace.

**Group classes** suit children who light up around other kids. Playing together teaches listening and keeping time, and it is a gentle way to try an instrument.

Some families start in a group, then switch to private lessons once a child is hooked. Online lessons can work for older children; young beginners usually do better with a teacher in the room. For weekly lessons in Glendale, a teacher close to home or school often beats one across the 134 at rush hour.

## Step 3: Judge the teacher in the first lesson

You learn more from one lesson than from any website. A good teacher tends to:

1. **Ask your child questions.** What music do you like? Why this instrument?
2. **Get the instrument into their hands early,** not after a long talk about theory.
3. **Check fit and posture:** how your child sits and holds the instrument.
4. **Teach something playable.** By the end, your child plays a short rhythm or tune.
5. **Send home one small task,** clear and specific.
6. **Talk to you at the end** about what to practice and how you can help.

Watch your child's face as you leave. Tired is fine. Proud is better. For more on reading a first session, see [what a great first class actually looks like](/blog/what-a-great-first-class-actually-looks-like-and-what-to-ask).

## Step 4: Rent, borrow or buy?

Violins and guitars come in sizes, and children outgrow them, so many families rent or borrow at first. For a keyboard, ask the teacher whether full-size keys matter.

Whatever you choose, ask the teacher to check it. A poorly set-up instrument, with strings too high or sticky keys, can make a child feel like the problem. Wait to buy anything expensive until lessons have stuck.

## Step 5: Practice at home without the fights

Practice is often the hardest part. A few habits help:

- **Short and often.** A few focused minutes most days beats one long Sunday session.
- **Same slot every day,** like after a snack or before screens.
- **Let your child choose the order.** Warm-up or favorite song first: their call.
- **Be the audience.** Ask for a mini concert. Notice what improved. Leave corrections to the teacher.
- **Let a bad day be short.** A frustrated five minutes is still practice. Stop on something they can play.

## Step 6: Getting ready for a recital

Many teachers hold recitals, where each student plays a short piece they know well. Nerves are normal. Help with a few living room rehearsals, practice the bow, and keep the morning calm. Ask early whether recitals are expected or optional, and what to wear.

## Signs it's working

- Your child plays or hums a piece without being asked.
- They want to show you something new.
- New pieces arrive every few weeks.
- Frustration comes and goes rather than building week after week.

## FAQ

### What is a good first instrument for a young child?

Piano and ukulele are common starting points with quick early wins. Violin suits patient children, drums suit energetic ones. Let your child help choose.

### Should my child take private or group music lessons?

Private lessons go at your child's pace and suit focused or shy kids. Group classes suit social kids and are a low-pressure way to try an instrument.

### How much should a child practice each day?

Short sessions most days work better than one long weekly session. Ask the teacher for a target that fits your child's age.

### Should we rent or buy a first instrument?

Many families rent or borrow at first, especially sized instruments like violin and guitar. Ask the teacher to check it first.
