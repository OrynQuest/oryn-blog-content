> **Key takeaways**
> - "Sensory-friendly" is mostly about the space; "autism-friendly" is also about how the class is taught.
> - A label is a starting point. Your questions show how a class really works.
> - Preparing your child, and planning an easy exit, can matter as much as the class you choose.

Every child experiences sound, light, touch and crowds differently. Some children seek out movement and noise; others need things quieter and more predictable. This guide is general information for parents, not medical or therapy advice. You and the people who know your child well are the real experts.

## What "sensory-friendly" and "autism-friendly" usually mean

The two terms overlap, but they are not the same.

- **Sensory-friendly** usually describes the environment. Think softer lighting, lower noise, fewer people, and a place to take a break. It can also mean flexibility about textures, like messy art materials or uniforms.
- **Autism-friendly** (sometimes called inclusive) usually goes further, into how the activity is run. Think a predictable routine, short and clear instructions, visual supports, and staff who are comfortable adjusting when a child needs something different.

Neither word has one fixed meaning. When a class uses one, treat it as a sign the vendor has thought about it, then ask how it works in practice.

## What to look for in the space

Southern California makes it easy to do activities outdoors much of the year. That can be wonderful for a child who needs room to move and fresh air. Outdoor spaces also bring things you cannot control: sun glare, wind, leaf blowers, traffic, other groups nearby, and hot afternoons. Indoor spaces have their own patterns. A big gym can echo. A dance studio can have mirrors and bright lights. A small art room can feel calm, or crowded.

When you look at photos, a video or the space itself, check:

- **Sound.** Is there background music? Can it be turned down or off? How much echo is there?
- **Light.** Bright overhead lights, natural light, or a mix? Is there anywhere dimmer?
- **Crowding.** How many children are usually in a session, and how close together?
- **A calm spot.** Is there a corner, bench or quiet room where a child can step away?
- **Smells and textures.** Paint, clay, cleaning products, mats, costumes. Small things can add up.
- **Access.** Is it step-free, with an accessible restroom, if your child or family needs that?

## How the class itself should feel

A comfortable room helps, but how the class runs often matters more. Signs that a class is built with different kinds of learners in mind:

- **A predictable start and end**, ideally the same each week.
- **A heads-up before transitions**, by voice, a visual schedule, a timer or a simple signal.
- **Instructions that are shown as well as said**, kept short.
- **Room for different kinds of participation.** Watching for a while can count as taking part.
- **Breaks without a spotlight**, so a child does not have to ask in front of the whole group.
- **Flexibility for parents**, such as staying nearby for the first few sessions.

## Questions to ask before you book

Patient, specific answers before you book are a good sign. Vague ones are worth noting.

Questions to ask:

1. What happens in the first ten minutes of a session?
2. Where can my child go if they need a break, and who goes with them?
3. Can I stay in the room or nearby for the first session?
4. Is music played, and can it be turned down?
5. How do you let children know a change is coming?
6. Have you adapted your class for autistic children or children with sensory differences before? What did that look like?
7. Can I see a photo or short video of the room before we come?
8. Is the space step-free, with an accessible restroom?

And things worth sharing with the vendor:

- What helps your child feel calm.
- What tends to overwhelm them, and the early signs.
- Any comfort items they bring, like headphones or a fidget.
- How your child communicates best.

## Preparing your child for a new activity

A little preparation can turn an unknown place into a known one.

- **Preview the space.** Look at listing photos or a video together, and talk through what will happen, in order.
- **Choose your arrival time.** Some children do well arriving early before it gets busy. Others do better walking in right at the start, with less waiting.
- **Pack a small kit.** Headphones, a snack, water and a comfort item.
- **Agree on an exit plan.** A signal your child can use, and a promise that leaving early is fine. A short first visit that ends well beats a long one that ends in overwhelm.
- **Celebrate the trying.** Praise showing up, not finishing.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between sensory-friendly and autism-friendly?

Sensory-friendly usually describes the environment: lower noise, softer light, fewer people and a place for breaks. Autism-friendly, or inclusive, usually also covers how the class is taught, like a predictable routine, visual supports and flexible participation. A class can aim for both, so ask how each one works.

### Is an indoor or outdoor activity better for a sensory-sensitive child?

It depends on the child. Outdoor spaces give room to move and fresh air, but bring glare, wind, heat and noises nobody controls. Indoor spaces are more predictable but can echo or feel bright. Think about where your child is calmest day to day.

### Should I tell the vendor about my child's needs before the first session?

It usually helps. Share what calms your child, what overwhelms them and the early signs to watch for. On ORYN Quest, the vendor sees your child's autism-friendly and sensory-support flags with a booking, but not medical notes, so add useful details by message.

### What if the first session is too much for my child?

Leave early if you need to and treat it as information, not failure. Talk with the vendor about what was hard. A shorter visit, a quieter time or a smaller group may help, or a different activity may simply fit your child better.
