> **Key takeaways**
> - Your family's starting point matters more than the language: a child who hears it at home needs a different class from a true beginner.
> - Little ones learn through songs, games and stories. Older kids are often ready for reading, writing and a clear plan.
> - Progress is real but uneven. Understanding usually comes before speaking.
> - Small habits at home do a lot of the work. Try one session before you commit to more.

In Long Beach you may hear many languages on a single block. For many families here, a second language is not a school subject. It is how a child talks to a grandparent or keeps up with a cousin.

This guide is for parents of kids aged about 3 to 14: choosing a language and a class style, what progress honestly looks like, and how to judge a class after one session.

## Where is your family starting from?

Place your child in one of these three groups first. The right class looks different for each.

### Your child hears the language at home

Your child understands a lot but answers in English. Look for a **heritage-speaker class**, built for kids who understand a language but need confidence speaking it, and later reading and writing it. A class for total beginners can bore these kids.

### Nobody at home speaks it

A beginner class fits, ideally one that spends most of its time in the language, with lots of repetition, songs and gestures.

### Your child speaks it but can't read or write it

Many kids chat easily with family but never learned the alphabet or characters. A literacy-focused class fills that gap. Ask whether reading starts right away or comes later.

## Picking a language

If your family has a heritage language, that is often the clear choice. A real person on the other end, like a grandmother on a video call, gives a child a reason to keep going.

If you are choosing fresh, ask:

- **Who will your child use it with?** Spanish is all around Southern California, so there are daily chances to hear it.
- **Does a friend or a school path point one way?** Some families pick Mandarin, Japanese or French for those reasons.
- **What is your child curious about?** A love of anime, a cooking show or a soccer team abroad is already a way in.

For Mandarin, ask whether the class teaches simplified or traditional characters, and whether it uses pinyin (spelling in Roman letters). Accents and words also vary by region, so tell the teacher which variety your family speaks.

## Four kinds of class

**Play-based immersion (about ages 3 to 6).** The teacher speaks the language almost the whole time, with songs, puppets, movement and picture books. No grammar drills. Kids often sing along before they speak on their own.

**Structured lessons (about ages 7 to 14).** Older kids like knowing what they are working toward. These classes mix conversation with reading, writing and some grammar. Look for a teacher who keeps it active with games and role plays, not only worksheets.

**Heritage-speaker classes.** These start from what kids already understand and often weave in family stories, holidays and food. For a child who has gone quiet in the family language, a room of kids with the same background can make speaking it feel normal again.

**Online or in person.** Young children usually do better in person, where they learn through movement and other kids. Online can suit older kids who focus well on a screen, and it opens up languages that are harder to find nearby.

## What progress really looks like

Language learning is slow and uneven. That is normal.

- **Understanding comes before speaking.** Many young children listen for a long time before they say much.
- **Mixing languages is common.** Kids learning two languages often use both in one sentence. It is part of the process.
- **Plateaus happen.** Quiet weeks are often followed by a sudden jump.
- **Confidence counts.** A child who answers a relative with one new phrase is making progress.

Be wary of promises of fluency by a set date. Every child is different, and how much a child hears and uses the language matters most.

## Small habits for home

You don't need to speak the language to help.

1. **Play songs in it** on the drive across town or at breakfast.
2. **Read picture books together.** Bilingual books help parents follow along.
3. **Switch a favorite show** to the language's audio track once a week.
4. **Let relatives lead** with a short weekly call in the family language.
5. **Ask your child to teach you** one word from class.

## Questions to ask a teacher

For more ways to compare, see [how to compare children's activity classes before you book](/blog/how-to-compare-children-s-activity-classes-before-you-book).

- How much of each class is in the language, and how much in English?
- Are kids grouped by age, by level, or both?
- Is the class for beginners, heritage speakers, or a mix?
- What does a typical session look like, start to finish?
- Is there anything to do at home between classes?
- For online classes: how long is each session, and how much does each child get to talk?

## Try one session, then decide

While you watch or wait, notice:

- Did the teacher use the language for most of the session?
- Did your child get to speak or sing, not just listen?
- Did your child come out relaxed, or proud of a new word?

## FAQ

### What age can kids start a language class?

Many classes for young children start around age 3 with songs and games. Structured classes with reading and writing often suit kids from about 7.

### What is a heritage-speaker class?

A class for kids who already hear a language at home. It builds on what they understand and helps them speak, read and write it.

### My child understands the language but won't speak it. Is that normal?

Yes, it is very common. Understanding usually comes before speaking. A heritage class, songs at home and patient relatives can help.
