> **Key takeaways**
> - STEM classes for kids come in five broad kinds: robotics, coding, engineering and building, science experiments, and math enrichment.
> - Match the kind of class to your child's temperament first, then check the age range.
> - Ask how big the group is, what your child takes home, and how progress shows.
> - Try one session, then decide together with your child.

On the Westside, "STEM class" can mean a robot that follows a line, a game built from code blocks, a craft-stick bridge that has to hold a stack of books, or a puzzle club for kids who love numbers. This guide is for parents of 5- to 14-year-olds who want to sort the options and pick one class worth trying.

## Five kinds of STEM class, side by side

| Kind of class | A typical session | Often suits a child who | What may come home |
|---|---|---|---|
| Robotics | Build a small machine, then program it to move | Takes things apart to see how they work | A video of the robot running |
| Coding | Make a game or animation step by step | Loves games and stories | A project to show the family |
| Engineering and building | Design, build, test, rebuild | Builds forts and towers at home | A model or a launcher |
| Science experiments | Guess, test, talk about why | Asks "why?" all day | Notes or an experiment to repeat |
| Math enrichment | Number games and logic puzzles | Enjoys spotting patterns | Puzzles for the kitchen table |

Many classes mix two or three of these. The listing's description shows where the weight falls.

## Start with temperament, then check the age

Your child's personality often tells you more than their age about which class will stick:

- **The tinkerer** always has a pile of parts nearby. Robotics and engineering let them build something real, then improve it.
- **The storyteller** invents characters and worlds. Coding classes that make games or animations give those stories somewhere to go.
- **The question-asker** wants to know why ice floats. Science experiment classes reward exactly that.
- **The puzzler** will happily work a logic problem for an hour. Math enrichment or coding fits well.
- **The mover** struggles to sit still. Look for building challenges where kids stand, test and try again, not a class spent at a desk.

Then check the age. Broadly, five- to seven-year-olds do well with short, hands-on tasks and show-and-tell. Around eight to ten, many kids can follow a multi-step build on their own. From eleven to fourteen, kids can usually take on longer projects, work in teams and stay with one problem for weeks. Reading level matters for coding and patience matters for robotics, so treat the listed age as a starting point.

## Project-based or screen-based?

**Project-based classes** put motors, wires, cardboard or test tubes in your child's hands. Most of the time goes to building and testing, which suits younger kids and anyone who learns by touching.

**Screen-based classes** spend most of the session on a laptop or tablet: writing code, designing in software, running simulations. These suit older kids who read and type comfortably.

Many good classes blend the two. If screen time is a worry, ask how much of a session is spent on a device.

Online classes skip the drive, but robotics and building usually work better in person, where kids can handle the parts.

## After school or on the weekend?

**After school** keeps STEM in the weekly rhythm, but by late afternoon some kids are done with sitting and thinking. A snack and a short break first helps. Westside afternoon traffic and pickup lines can stretch a short drive, so time the route at the hour you would really leave.

**Weekend sessions** often run longer, and kids arrive fresher, which suits big builds. They also compete with beach days, sports and family plans, so pick a time your family can protect. Either way, check the school calendar for early-release days and breaks.

## Questions to ask before you book

1. **How many kids are in a group, and how many adults?** The answer tells you how quickly a teacher can reach a stuck child.
2. **What will my child take home or show?** A model, a project link or photos of the build. Kids who leave with something talk about class at dinner.
3. **How will I see progress?** Harder builds, new tools or a demo at the end of each unit. A clear answer means there is a plan.
4. **What happens when a child gets stuck?** Listen for patience, not "they just figure it out".

## Signs of a good STEM teacher

- **Asks more than tells.** "What do you think will happen?" comes before the answer.
- **Treats mistakes as part of the work.** A robot that drives into a wall is a clue, not a failure.
- **Gets every child's hands on it.** In pairs or teams, each child builds, not just watches a confident partner.
- **Ends with show-and-tell.** A few minutes to share gives kids a reason to finish.

## Try one session, then decide

You don't need a full term to learn whether a class fits. After one session, watch: does your child explain what they built without being asked, try it again at home, ask when they can go back? For more on judging that first visit, see [what a great first class actually looks like](/blog/what-a-great-first-class-actually-looks-like-and-what-to-ask).

If it's a miss, change the kind of class before giving up on STEM. A child who drifted off in coding may light up building a bridge.

## FAQ

### What age can kids start STEM classes?

Many STEM classes start around age five with hands-on building and simple experiments. Coding and longer robotics projects usually come later, once a child reads comfortably and can stay with a task.

### Is robotics or coding a better fit for my child?

It depends on the child. Kids who like building with their hands often enjoy robotics, where code makes something real move. Kids who love games and stories may prefer coding. One session of each tells you a lot.

### Should a kids' STEM class be mostly on a screen?

Not necessarily. Younger kids usually do better with hands-on projects, and many good classes mix planning, building and programming. Ask the vendor how much of a session is spent on a device.
