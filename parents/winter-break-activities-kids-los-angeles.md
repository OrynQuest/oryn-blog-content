> **Key takeaways**
> - Winter break is long. Plan it in October or November, not the week before.
> - Give each week of the break its own job instead of planning day by day.
> - One short camp or a few drop-ins, plus family days, is usually enough structure.
> - A light routine and a class given as a gift can make the whole break calmer.

In many Los Angeles schools, winter break lasts about two weeks, but the exact days differ from district to district, so check your own school's calendar first. Whatever your dates, the break lands in the busiest part of the year, when work, travel and holiday errands all pile up. That is why the best time to plan it is now.

## Why plan in October or November

A two-week break is hard to handle on the fly. Planning early gives you three things:

- **Time to talk it over.** Ask your kids what they would like to do. A child who helped choose a camp or class goes in excited instead of reluctant.
- **Time to line up help.** Grandparents, a friend's family or a sitter can often cover a day or two if you ask before their own plans fill up.
- **Real choices.** Plan the week before and you take what is left. Plan ahead and you choose.

## Your planning timeline

**In October:** find your school's break dates and note any days your work is closed. Ask your kids for two or three things they would love to do. Decide whether you are traveling and roughly when.

**In November:** look at classes, camps and drop-ins that might fit, and save the ones you like. Message vendors with questions. Talk to the other adults in your child's life about which days they can help.

**In early December:** confirm what you are doing and add it to the family calendar. Plan the first and last days too.

## A simple week-by-week plan for the break

Instead of planning each day, give each part of the break a job.

**Week one: the active week.** Many parents still work for part of this week, and kids arrive with lots of energy after the last day of school. This is a good week for a short camp or a few drop-in classes.

**Week two: the family week.** This is often when holidays, visits and travel happen. Keep it light. Let family time, slow mornings and simple outings fill the days. One activity a day, at most, is plenty.

**The last two or three days: the reset.** Earlier bedtimes, a quiet day at home and packed backpacks ease everyone back toward school.

Your weeks may fall in a different order. The idea is one purpose per stretch, so you are not making a new plan every morning.

## Mix a short camp or a few drop-ins with family days

Nothing scheduled can feel endless by day four. Everything scheduled wears everyone out. A mix tends to work best.

- **A short camp** gives a few days in a row the same rhythm, which suits kids who like to know what comes next.
- **Drop-in classes** fill single days. They are a low-pressure way to try something new, like cooking, art, music or a sport.
- **Online classes** can help on a day you are packing, waiting on a delivery or hosting guests.
- **Family days** are the point of the break for many families. Leave several of them unplanned on purpose.

## Keep a light routine

Kids don't need a school schedule over the break, but a few steady habits help everyone:

- **Keep wake-up and bedtime roughly the same.** A later bedtime is fine; a completely different one makes the return to school harder.
- **Have one daily anchor.** A morning walk, a reading time after lunch or a family dinner gives each day a shape.
- **Agree on screen time before the break starts,** not in the middle of it.

## Plan around holiday crowds and travel

Popular outings, shopping areas and roads can be more crowded than usual in late December, and travel days rarely go to plan.

- **Go early.** Outings in the morning are usually calmer than the afternoon.
- **Choose weekdays when you can.** Weekends during the holidays are often the busiest days.
- **Leave the day after travel open.** Everyone is tired. Don't book anything that needs an early start.
- **Keep a home-day list ready** for when plans fall through: a board game, a baking project, a puzzle or a movie afternoon.

## Give an experience as a gift

If your house is already full of toys, a class can make a thoughtful present. A child who loves to draw might enjoy an art session. A young cook might like a cooking class. Wrap something small that goes with it, like an apron or a sketchbook, and add a card that explains what the gift is. It also gives your child something to look forward to after the holidays.

## Budget for the break in general terms

Care, outings, gifts and travel all arrive in the same few weeks. A few habits help:

- **Decide early which days actually need paid care or activities,** and which can be family days.
- **Set one amount for the whole break,** rather than deciding day by day.
- **Mix in free and low-cost days.** Parks, libraries and home projects are part of a good break. For more ideas, see our [weekend activities guide](/blog/weekend-activities-for-kids-on-any-budget-2026-parent-s).

## FAQ

### When is winter break for Los Angeles schools?

It depends on your school district or school. Many schools give about two weeks around the holidays, but start and end dates differ, so check your school's published calendar before you plan anything.

### How early should I plan winter break activities?

October or November is a good time to start. Find your dates first, ask your kids what they would enjoy, and make a shortlist. Confirm your plans in early December.

### How many activities should kids do over winter break?

There is no right number. Many families find that a short camp or a few drop-in classes in one week, plus unplanned family days, gives enough structure without wearing everyone out.

### Is a class a good holiday gift for a child?

It can be, especially for a child with plenty of toys. Pick something tied to what they love and explain the gift in a card.
